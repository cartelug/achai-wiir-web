A line of tricycles, and what it means to be seen

Before the tricycles, the hardest part of the day wasn't distance — it was being planned around, rather than planned for. Composite, illustrative account.

Working editorial draft — composite, illustrative narrative. Not verbatim quotations from a named individual.

The morning the tricycles arrived in Aweil, the queue formed the way queues do everywhere — a little uneven, a little impatient, entirely ordinary. That ordinariness is worth pausing on, because ordinary is not usually the word attached to disability and aid in the same sentence. What follows is a composite scene, built for this site around the verified fact of the January 2023 distribution; it is not a transcript of any single person's day, and no individual quoted here is a real, identified person — treat it as illustrative until the Foundation can supply and approve real, consented testimony.

For a person with a mobility disability in a town like Aweil, distance is never just distance. It's the market that's technically two kilometres away and practically a different country; it's the clinic visit that costs a favour and a wait for someone with a working vehicle; it's showing up late to things that started without you because getting there took three times as long as it should have. A tricycle doesn't fix any of that on its own. What it changes is who has to ask whom, and how often.

That's the part easy to miss if the story is told as a headline number — 98 tricycles — and nothing else. Ninety-eight is a real, sourced figure, reported independently by Radio Tamazuj, and it belongs on this site exactly as reported. But a number on its own can flatten what it represents into an abstraction, and the people who received these tricycles are not an abstraction. Each one is someone who, on an ordinary Tuesday after the distribution, could plan a trip to the market on their own schedule instead of someone else's.

The same event, reported alongside the tricycle distribution, included food packages for blind and deaf community groups in the area. It's tempting to read that as an afterthought to the headline number, but disability is not one condition with one fix, and a program that only ever addresses mobility while ignoring sensory access is solving half a problem. Whether that pairing reflects a deliberate, integrated approach to disability inclusion, or two separate decisions that happened to land on the same day, isn't something this site can verify from the published report alone — it's a fair question for the Foundation to answer on the record.

There is also a discrepancy worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over: an earlier planning reference described “100 motorised wheelchairs,” while the published, independently reported figure is 98 tricycles. Those are two different kinds of device serving two different needs, and the gap between the plan and the report is exactly the kind of thing a credible record should flag rather than quietly reconcile. This site uses the reported figure and terminology, and says so, because a maybe-impressive unverified number is worth less than an exact, sourced one.

What's harder to capture in any account, composite or otherwise, is the specific, unglamorous relief of not having to ask for a ride. Dignity, in a lot of disability-inclusion work, gets talked about as a value statement — a word on a mission page. In Aweil, on one January morning, it looked like a line of tricycles and a queue that, however ordinary, ended with more people able to leave under their own power than arrived able to.

Context & verification

Verified fact: 98 tricycles were distributed to persons with disabilities in Aweil, plus food packages for blind and deaf community groups — reported by Radio Tamazuj, January 2023. Read the source report ↗
